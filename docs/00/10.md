## Adding Social Structure to Your Rabbit Simulation

Social structure introduces interactions among rabbits that go beyond simple competition for food. It can lead to emergent group behaviors, hierarchies, and collective strategies. Here are several ways to incorporate social structure into your Mesa model, with implementation ideas and expected effects.

---

### 1. **Group Formation (Herding / Schooling)**

Rabbits in the wild often live in groups for safety and foraging efficiency. You can implement a tendency for rabbits to move toward nearby conspecifics.

**Implementation ideas:**
- Add a `group_id` or `group_attraction` parameter. In each step, a rabbit’s movement decision could be influenced by the positions of nearby rabbits (e.g., move toward the centroid of neighbors within a certain radius).
- Use a rule: if a rabbit sees at least X other rabbits within distance Y, it stays with the group; otherwise, it wanders randomly.
- Create a `Group` agent that holds references to its members and coordinates movement (e.g., all members move together).

**Effects:**
- Rabbits cluster, which may lead to localized overgrazing but also better detection of predators (if added later).
- Group living could reduce individual predation risk (dilution effect) but increase competition for grass.

---

### 2. **Dominance Hierarchies**

In many animal societies, individuals establish dominance relationships that affect access to resources (e.g., food, mates). You can assign each rabbit a dominance score (e.g., based on age, size, energy, or a genetic trait) and use it to resolve conflicts.

**Implementation ideas:**
- Add a `dominance` attribute (float) that can be inherited with mutation.
- When two rabbits meet on the same cell and both want to eat the same grass, the dominant one eats first (or gets a larger share). The subordinate may be displaced.
- Dominance could affect reproduction: dominant males might have higher mating success.

**Effects:**
- Unequal resource distribution leads to variation in survival and reproduction, potentially driving evolution of traits that increase dominance.
- May create a stable social order, reducing constant fighting (if modeled as energy cost for losers).

---

### 3. **Territoriality**

Rabbits (especially some species) defend territories against intruders. You can implement a system where rabbits claim and defend areas.

**Implementation ideas:**
- Each rabbit (or group) has a home range or territory center. They mark it by depositing scent (a variable on grid cells) and defend against intruders.
- When a rabbit enters a cell marked by another, there is a probability of conflict: the intruder may be chased away (losing energy) or the resident may be displaced.
- Territory size could be linked to energy needs or genetic disposition.

**Effects:**
- Spatial segregation of rabbits, reducing direct competition but creating empty patches.
- Could lead to source-sink dynamics if some areas are more productive.

---

### 4. **Communication (Alarm Calls)**

Rabbits are known to thump or make sounds to warn others of danger. You can model simple communication that enhances group survival.

**Implementation ideas:**
- When a rabbit detects a predator (if added), it emits an alarm signal that spreads to nearby rabbits (within a radius). Those rabbits then have a higher chance of fleeing or hiding.
- The signal could have a cost (energy) for the caller, and a genetic basis for tendency to call.
- Alternatively, rabbits could leave pheromone trails to indicate food sources.

**Effects:**
- Cooperative behavior emerges; groups with more callers might survive better.
- Evolution of altruism if callers put themselves at risk (trade-off).

---

### 5. **Cooperative Breeding / Alloparenting**

In some rabbit species, non-breeding individuals help raise the young. You could add a helper system.

**Implementation ideas:**
- When a rabbit reproduces, offspring could be cared for by the mother and possibly other rabbits in the same group (helpers).
- Helpers might gain indirect fitness benefits (kin selection) if they are related. You could track relatedness using genetic markers.
- Offspring survival could depend on the number of helpers present.

**Effects:**
- Increases juvenile survival, potentially boosting population growth.
- May select for kin recognition and altruistic behavior.

---

### 6. **Social Learning and Information Transmission**

Rabbits could learn from each other about good foraging spots or migration routes.

**Implementation ideas:**
- Maintain a simple "knowledge" value per rabbit (e.g., best location known). When rabbits meet, they can exchange information (copy the better location).
- Movement decisions could be influenced by socially acquired information, combined with personal experience.

**Effects:**
- Faster adaptation to changing environments (e.g., if grass regrowth patterns shift).
- Cultural evolution on top of genetic evolution.

---

### 7. **Mating Systems and Pair Bonding**

If you introduce genders (male/female), you can model pair bonding, mate choice, and family groups.

**Implementation ideas:**
- Rabbits have a gender attribute (inherited). Mating occurs when a male and female meet and both have sufficient energy.
- They could form temporary or permanent pairs. Offspring might stay with parents for a few steps before dispersing.
- Mate choice based on genetic traits (e.g., females prefer males with certain speed genes) can drive sexual selection.

**Effects:**
- Adds sexual reproduction dynamics (already hinted in genetics). Population genetics become more realistic.
- Can lead to sexual dimorphism and interesting evolutionary trajectories.

---

### 8. **Social Status and Roles**

Within a group, rabbits might have specialized roles (e.g., sentinel, forager). You could implement a simple role allocation based on traits.

**Implementation ideas:**
- Rabbits with high vigilance (a new trait) are more likely to act as sentinels, watching for danger while others feed.
- Roles could be flexible and change based on context or energy levels.

**Effects:**
- Division of labor improves group efficiency but may create dependencies.

---

## Practical Considerations for Implementation in Mesa

- **Agent interactions**: Use the grid to detect neighbors (`model.grid.get_neighbors`) or use a `ContinuousSpace` if you want more precise distances.
- **Group membership**: Store a reference to a group object in each rabbit, or use a dictionary mapping group IDs to lists of agents.
- **Communication**: You can use a `model.signal` dictionary that stores alarm flags for cells, or propagate messages through agent steps.
- **Conflict resolution**: Simple if-else rules based on dominance values, possibly with a random element.
- **Data collection**: Track group sizes, territory sizes, frequency of conflicts, etc.

Start with one or two social features and observe how they change the dynamics. Social structure often interacts with other aspects like genetics and resource distribution, leading to rich, emergent patterns.
