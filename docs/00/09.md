## Incorporate Learning or Adaptation

Learning and adaptation allow agents to adjust their behavior or traits based on experience, leading to more realistic dynamics and the emergence of complex strategies. In your rabbit simulation, you can introduce several forms of learning and adaptation, ranging from simple memory to social learning and enhanced evolution.

### 1. Individual Learning

Individual learning means a rabbit changes its behavior based on its own past experiences. This can be implemented in various ways:

#### a. Spatial Memory
Rabbits could remember where they found grass and return to those locations.
- **Implementation**: Give each rabbit a memory list or dictionary mapping positions to a “goodness” score (e.g., last time grass was found there, or number of times eaten). When moving, they can bias their choice toward neighboring cells with higher scores.
- **Example**:
  ```python
  self.memory[self.pos] = self.model.schedule.steps  # remember last visit
  # When choosing a move, weight cells by recency of grass
  ```

#### b. Reinforcement Learning
Treat each action (e.g., moving in a direction, staying) as having a value based on the energy gained. Rabbits update these values using a simple learning rule like Q-learning.
- **Implementation**: Store a Q-table for each rabbit (or a neural network). After moving and eating, update the value of the previous state-action pair based on the reward (energy gain).
- **Trade-off**: Exploration vs. exploitation—rabbits occasionally try random actions to discover better strategies.

#### c. Operant Conditioning
If a rabbit finds grass repeatedly in a certain habitat type (e.g., cells with high regrowth), it could develop a preference for similar cells.
- **Implementation**: Keep a moving average of energy obtained per habitat type (e.g., based on grass regrowth time of that cell). Use this average to bias movement toward similar habitats.

#### d. Avoidance Learning
If a rabbit nearly dies (energy very low) in a certain area, it might avoid that area in the future.
- **Implementation**: Associate negative outcomes with locations and reduce their attractiveness.

### 2. Social Learning

Rabbits can learn from others, accelerating adaptation.

#### a. Copying Successful Individuals
Rabbits could observe and imitate the behavior or even the genes of neighbors who have high energy.
- **Implementation**: When two rabbits meet, the one with lower energy may copy the movement probabilities or habitat preferences of the higher-energy rabbit.
- **Cultural Evolution**: This can lead to the spread of advantageous behaviors without genetic change.

#### b. Information Sharing
Rabbits could communicate the location of food sources or warn about dangers (if predators are added).
- **Implementation**: If a rabbit finds grass, it could leave a pheromone trail (increase attractiveness of that cell for others) or emit a signal that nearby rabbits can detect.
- **Alarm Calls**: If a predator is nearby, rabbits could emit a warning that makes others flee.

#### c. Teaching
More advanced: older rabbits could actively lead younger ones to good feeding grounds.

### 3. Enhanced Evolutionary Adaptation

Your model already includes genetic traits and mutation. You can extend this to include learning-related genes.

#### a. Evolve Learning Parameters
Make learning rate, memory capacity, or exploration tendency heritable traits subject to mutation and selection.
- **Example**: A gene `learning_rate` determines how quickly a rabbit updates its Q-values. Rabbits that learn too slowly may be outcompeted.

#### b. Gene–Culture Coevolution
Allow genetic traits to influence social learning (e.g., a gene for being a "conformist" vs. "innovator"), and let socially learned behaviors feed back into genetic fitness.

#### c. Baldwin Effect
Learned behaviors can become genetically assimilated over generations if they consistently improve fitness. You could model this by having a learned preference become part of the genotype after many generations (though this is complex).

### 4. Phenotypic Plasticity

Phenotypic plasticity is the ability of an individual to change its physiology or behavior in response to environmental conditions without genetic change.

#### a. Metabolic Adjustment
Rabbits could lower their metabolism when food is scarce (hibernation-like state) to conserve energy.
- **Implementation**: If energy drops below a threshold, reduce `gene_metabolism` temporarily (until energy recovers).

#### b. Developmental Plasticity
The environment experienced early in life could shape adult traits. For example, rabbits born in low-food environments might develop lower metabolism or higher efficiency.

#### c. Learning to Forage Efficiently
As rabbits gain experience, they could become more efficient at extracting energy from grass (e.g., reducing the time spent eating). This could be modeled as an increasing "skill" that reduces metabolism or increases energy gain per grass.

### Implementation Considerations

- **Memory storage**: Each rabbit may need additional attributes (e.g., a dictionary of visited cells, Q-table, etc.). Be mindful of memory usage if the grid is large.
- **Scalability**: Learning algorithms can be computationally expensive. Keep them simple initially (e.g., recency-weighted averaging) and iterate.
- **Temporal scale**: Learning happens within a rabbit’s lifetime, whereas evolution happens across generations. You can observe both timescales interacting.
- **Data collection**: To study learning, track metrics like average energy gain per step, exploration rate, or the emergence of preferred locations.

### Potential Research Questions with Learning

- Does spatial memory help rabbits survive longer in a patchy environment?
- Can social learning lead to the rapid spread of beneficial behaviors, and does it slow down genetic evolution?
- How does the trade-off between exploration and exploitation affect population dynamics?
- Do learning individuals outcompete non-learners, and if so, under what conditions?

By incorporating learning and adaptation, your simulation becomes a rich platform for exploring how behavior and evolution interact in ecological systems.
